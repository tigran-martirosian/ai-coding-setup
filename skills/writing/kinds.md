# Notes by kind of text

Read the section for the kind you are writing. Where the person's own samples or the project's writing rule
say otherwise, they win.

## Email

The point or the ask in the first two lines; the reader decides from those whether to read on. One topic per
email. Plain text throughout, the subject line too (`Subject: ...`, never in bold): paragraphs, no headings or
bold; a short list only for steps or several dates. Close with the next step and who takes it ("I can do
Tuesday or Thursday afternoon"), then the name the user gave you, or a `[name]` blank when they gave none (a
name read from a folder path or an account is a guess). Match the other side's register: a
two-line reply gets a short answer. Cut the warm-up sentence and the sign-off line that says nothing.

## Short note or message

Write it the way the person texts: their length, their capitals, their punctuation. One message, one ask. No
greeting line unless they use one. Facts in the message, never "see above" or a reference the reader lacks.

## README and the About line

The About line (the one under the repo name) says what the thing is and who it is for, in under about 120
characters, with no adjectives that rate it. The README answers, in this order: what this is, why the reader
would want it (what they can do with it that they couldn't before), and how to start, in the fewest steps that
work, then one real example with its output. Name what it doesn't do and what it needs. A few features, the
ones a newcomer would pick it for, only after the reader knows what the thing is; the full list belongs on a
features page. Badges, emoji and a "Why X?" section are optional and often noise.

## Tour, guides and explainers

A tour shows a newcomer the few things worth trying first, each as what they get and how to try it. A guide is
the task the reader came to do, in order, with the real command or setting and what they should see after it.
An explainer answers how something works and why it is built that way, in the reader's terms, with one
example. Each opens on what the reader gets from it, never on how it came to be.

## Wiki page for colleagues

What a colleague needs to use or change the thing: what it is for, where it lives, how to run it, who owns it,
the one or two traps. History, the options weighed and the dead ends go to a linked note, if anywhere.

## Release notes

What the reader can now do or no longer hits, in their words, the biggest first; leave out internal renames,
what stayed the same, and fixes to things that never shipped. Each line is a change they would notice.

## Description of work done (a PR, a report)

For the person who reads it later without context. One line of what changed for them, a short paragraph of
why, how it was checked (the command and its result), and what a reviewer should look at first. The few
changes that matter, not every one made: an edge case tried and then removed was never there for the reader.

## Film script

One message, one benefit up front, and the two or three things a viewer should remember; the full feature list
stays on the website. Each shot shows the thing working, with the line spoken or shown over it.

## Resume line

One line per result: the verb, what was done, and the number or the named outcome ("cut the nightly build
from 40 to 12 minutes"). The person's real numbers only; when one is missing, ask, or write the line without
it. No "passionate", "results-driven" or "spearheaded"; a plain verb reads stronger.

## LinkedIn and other profiles

A profile is an introduction, not the resume. A GitHub profile or a short bio: who the person is, what they
work on now in one sentence, and one short line per project saying what it is. Numbers, costs, counts and how a
thing works stay in the resume and the project READMEs. Before drafting one, read three or four profiles of known
people in the field and match their length.

First person, the way the person talks. The headline says what they do and for whom. The About section: what
they work on now, one or two things they did with the result, and what they want next. Short paragraphs; no
emoji bullets, no "Here's what I learned", no closing question unless they write that way.

## Form answers and cover letters

Answer the question that was asked, in its first sentence. One specific example beats three claims. A cover
letter: why this role at this place (a fact about them, not praise), the one or two things from the person's
record that fit it, and the ask. Keep within the stated length. Never invent a reason, an experience or a
feeling the person did not give you; ask instead.

## Posts

One idea per post. Open on the fact, the result or the question itself, not a run-up. Short paragraphs are
fine; a line break after every sentence is a template. End when the point is made; a question at the end is
fine when the person really wants answers. Hashtags and emoji only as the person uses them.
